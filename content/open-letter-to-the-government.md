title: Open Letter to the Government
---
# To the Home Secretary, the Secretary of State for Science, Innovation and Technology, and all Members of Parliament

I am writing with a sense of urgency and deep concern about the proposed social media restrictions and the broader push toward internet age-gating legislation currently under consideration. This is not a dismissal of the real challenges posed by online harms. It is a plea for evidence-based policy — because the measures being proposed will not achieve their stated goals, and they will cause significant, avoidable harm to some of the most vulnerable people in this country.

I ask you to consider the evidence before proceeding.

## I. The Ban Will Not Work

The central premise of age-gating social media — that restricting access will meaningfully reduce harm — is not supported by the available evidence. Blanket bans conflate restricted access with safer access, framing young people as vulnerable objects for protection rather than as rights-bearing participants in digital society. The logic assumes that reducing young people's exposure will automatically reduce digital harms, but this ignores the structural conditions that produce online harms — inadequate content moderation, predatory advertising, and algorithmic design — which are far more harmful to users regardless of age.

Australia became the first country to ban under-16s from social media in 2024. Early evidence suggests the policy is not working as intended: a first-of-its-kind study found that 61 per cent of Australian 12 to 15-year-olds who previously had social media accounts still have access to at least one platform. The National Youth Agency, the statutory body for youth work in England, has noted that there is "little evidential basis from countries attempting similar bans, of young people being safer, and indeed we know that young people already find routes around age restrictions." Prohibition risks allowing major platforms and perpetrators of abuse to evade responsibility for harmful content, while pushing young people toward darker, less regulated corners of the internet where there is a greater risk of harm.

When TikTok was banned on government devices in 2023, usage on personal devices among the same demographic did not decline — it shifted. The policy challenge should not be whether young people's online participation should be made illegal, but how to support that participation.

Furthermore, age verification technology itself remains fundamentally unreliable. Age estimation systems often use sensitive data like biometrics, which are prone to errors and bias. Biometric-based approaches can be biased based on gender, race, or disability. Facial-recognition systems are notoriously unreliable for marginalized communities, with high error rates particularly when estimating age across race and gender lines. A 2018 MIT study found that commercial facial recognition technology had an error rate of up to 34% for dark-skinned women, compared to a maximum of 0.8% for light-skinned men. For trans and non-binary people, misidentification rates can reach 100%. The result is a regime that will either be trivially bypassed or will lock out legitimate users while creating vast new databases of sensitive personal information — a target-rich environment for data breaches.

The Online Safety Act 2023 already provides a framework for platform accountability. Under Part 5 of the Act, providers have a duty to ensure, by the use of age verification or age estimation, that children are protected. Adding access restriction on top of duty-of-care obligations does not add marginal benefit; it adds marginal risk.

## II. The Ban Will Harm LGBTQ+ People

One cannot discuss restricting access to social media without reckoning with what social media means for LGBTQ+ people, particularly young people. The evidence here is not ambiguous.

The Trevor Project's 2023 National Survey on LGBTQ Youth Mental Health — which surveyed over 28,000 LGBTQ+ young people — found that those who reported feeling safe and understood in at least one online space had 20% lower odds of attempting suicide in the past year and 15% lower odds of recent anxiety. A 2024 survey by the Trevor Project found that 67% of LGBTQ+ teens said they have found affirming spaces and communities online, compared with 46% who said the same of their school and 31% who said so about their home. This is not incidental. This is a lifeline.

Research published in *Social Media + Society* (2021) developed a Social Media Benefits Scale through a survey of over 6,000 LGBTQ+ youth and found that social media participation can enhance well-being through identity work, peer support, and emotional regulation. For young people, social media platforms are sites of social participation, identity-formation, information access, and civic engagement. A ban does not distinguish between predatory content and peer support. It does not distinguish between harmful material and a 15-year-old asking an anonymous forum whether what they are feeling is normal. The blunt instrument of age restriction sweeps away both.

The Equalities Impact Assessment published alongside recent proposed measures is concerningly thin on this point. I urge the government to commission a full, independent assessment of the impact of any access restriction measures on LGBTQ+ young people's mental health and access to support, drawing on the substantial body of existing research.

## III. The Ban Will Harm Abuse Survivors

There is a second vulnerable cohort that is consistently overlooked in these debates: survivors of domestic abuse, sexual violence, and childhood maltreatment. For many of these individuals, social media and online platforms provide essential pathways to support, information, and safety.

Research from Refuge found that one in three UK women (36%) have experienced online abuse perpetrated on social media or other online platforms at some point in their lives. The Domestic Abuse Commissioner has called for the Online Safety Bill to be more robust when it comes to domestic abuse, noting that survivors have experienced harassment, stalking, monitoring, threats of violence, intimate image abuse, hacking of accounts, and online impersonation on social media and other platforms. Imposing additional verification or access barriers risks severing these connections.

Age-gating that requires persistent identity verification, biometric data, or linked accounts will disproportionately harm those who cannot safely maintain a stable digital identity — including survivors in refuges, those with no fixed address, and those fleeing coercive control. Transgender individuals may find themselves misidentified as having "synthetic identities," potentially leading to exclusion from vital services like banking and housing.

For children whose offline environments are the source of their trauma, the online world is not a luxury — it is a door.

## IV. The Ban Will Limit Teenagers' Political Education and Civic Engagement

There is a third dimension to this debate that has received far too little attention: the damage these restrictions will do to young people's political education and democratic participation. This is not a peripheral concern — it goes to the heart of what kind of democracy we are building.

The government is currently moving to lower the voting age to 16 and 17 through the Elections Bill, intending to engage young voters early and build the foundations for lifelong democratic participation. This reform will give 1.5 million 16 and 17-year-olds the vote. Yet at the same time, the government is proposing to ban or severely restrict their access to the very platforms where political information and discourse now predominantly occur. The contradiction could not be starker. As the Conservative Party has pointed out, 16 and 17-year-olds are old enough to choose a government or an MP, but will face a social media curfew so won't be easily able to check the result of that vote.

For young people, social media is not merely entertainment — it is where they learn about politics. Research with more than 3,000 young people in year ten (ages 14 and 15) in schools across England found that 75% said they learned most about social and political issues online, including on social media. Teens see social media, more than school, as the place to learn about social and political issues. The National Youth Agency has warned that "as the voting age moves to 16, a ban on social media would also hinder their readiness to participate meaningfully in democratic processes."

Young people themselves understand this. Nadia Mackiewicz, a 17-year-old from Northern Ireland, told the BBC: "A lot of the volunteering stuff I have found has actually been through social media. I feel like banning social media completely for young people can actually limit political engagement." Another young person quoted in the same BBC article noted that back when they were 14 was when they "began to understand the wider impact of social media in a social sense, with being able to talk to classmates, talk to friends and even gain new friends through social media."

The Information Technology and Innovation Foundation has warned that a ban would "sever millions of teenagers from essential spaces for communication, creativity, civic engagement, and access to information, while doing little to address the real sources of harm online." Professor Ariadne Vromen and Dr Maia Almeida-Amir of the University of Glasgow have argued that restricting access risks overemphasising protection to the detriment of young people's rights to free information, expression, association, and political participation, as identified in the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child.

A 2026 study on the Online Safety Act found that OSA-related political discourse rose by over 200% among UK-based users following the Act's passage. This demonstrates that online platforms are where political engagement is happening. Cutting young people off from these spaces at the very moment they are gaining the right to vote is not protection — it is disenfranchisement.

We may soon live in a world in which 16-year-olds will have the vote but won't be permitted to research the issues on which they're voting. That is not a recipe for an informed electorate. It is a recipe for a generation disengaged from the democratic process at the very moment they are invited to participate in it.

## V. A Better Alternative: Education and DNS-Level Filtering

If the goal is genuinely to protect children and vulnerable users from harmful content, there is a path forward that is both more effective and less harmful. It has two components, both of which are supported by evidence.

### A. Parental and Educational Empowerment

The most consistent predictor of safe online behaviour in minors is not a technological barrier — it is engaged, informed parenting combined with school-based digital literacy education. A literature review from the University of Bath found that although restrictive approaches are the most common strategy employed by parents, this type of mediation is largely ineffective in reducing online risks. A review of the effectiveness of parental controls reveals mixed results: some uses bring benefits to children's safety, but others have no effect or limit children's opportunities, and some have adverse effects.

Rather than isolated measures, action must sit within a comprehensive, whole-system package of support combining agile regulation, effective digital education, and an ecosystem of trusted adults to help young people make informed, safe decisions. The National Youth Agency has argued that "sustained investment in youth work and digital education that reaches every young person would create real, lasting protection and confidence."

The government should also embed digital citizenship and media literacy in schools — equip young people with the skills to navigate, question and participate confidently online.

**I recommend:**

- A statutory requirement for age-appropriate digital literacy education in all primary and secondary schools, from Year 1 onward.
- A national public health campaign aimed at parents and carers, focused on practical strategies for managing children's online lives rather than fear-based messaging.
- Funding for community-based parent support groups, particularly in deprived areas, to build collective digital confidence.

### B. Opt-In DNS-Level Content Filtering

At the network level, there exists a technically mature, privacy-preserving, and entirely opt-in approach: DNS filtering. The UK already operates a voluntary system through the four major ISPs (BT, Sky, TalkTalk, Virgin Media), covering over 90% of residential broadband connections.

However, we should be cautious about overstating the effectiveness of filtering. A 2025 study from the University of Twente evaluating seven parental control solutions — including router-based filters, DNS-based services, and software tools — found that parental controls frequently fall short of providing adequate protection. Some systems fail to block up to 96% of sensitive domains, while others block appropriate content or apply inconsistent and arbitrary filtering rules.

The advantage of DNS filtering is not that it is a silver bullet — it is that it is:

- **Opt-in** — It respects the autonomy of families to make their own decisions.
- **Granular** — Categories can be independently toggled.
- **Auditable** — Parents can review blocked content with their children, facilitating conversation rather than prohibition.
- **Non-identifying** — Unlike age verification at the platform level, DNS filtering does not require users to submit identity documents, biometric data, or payment information.

**I recommend:**

- An expansion and standardisation of the current ISP-level filtering to include clearer information at point of sign-up and an annual reminder for existing customers.
- A free, standalone DNS service published by the UK government that any family can configure on their home router, with clear documentation and helpline support.
- Explicit recognition that filtering should be a complement to — not a replacement for — education and parental engagement.

## VI. Conclusion

The social media ban does not address the problem it claims to solve. The evidence on restriction measures is consistent: bans lack solid scientific support, they are unlikely to eliminate or change social media use among youth in positive ways, and they risk unintended consequences. Worse, the ban will actively harm those it purports to protect. It will sever a primary lifeline for LGBTQ+ young people who have nowhere else to turn. It will cut off abuse survivors from the support networks they depend on. It will limit the political education of young people at the very moment they are being given the right to vote. It will do this on the basis of a framework that has not been adequately assessed for its equalities impact.

There is a better way. Invest in digital literacy education. Empower parents through transparent, opt-in tools. Hold platforms accountable through the Online Safety Act's duty-of-care framework — not through blunt access restrictions that abandon the most vulnerable to the most unregulated corners of the internet.

I do not oppose protecting children. I oppose measures that will not work, that will cause documented harm, and that ignore the evidence that sits in plain sight. I ask you to read the studies. I ask you to think again.

**ignoblia**

June 2026
