---
title: An Open Letter to the Government
---

**To the Home Secretary, the Secretary of State for Science, Innovation and Technology, and all Members of Parliament,**

We are writing to you with a sense of urgency and deep concern regarding the proposed social media restrictions and the broader push toward internet age-gating legislation currently under consideration. This letter is not a dismissal of the real challenges posed by online harms. It is a plea for evidence-based policy — because the measures being proposed will not achieve their stated goals, and they will cause significant, avoidable harm to some of the most vulnerable people in this country.

We ask you to consider the evidence before proceeding.

---

## I. The Ban Will Not Work

The central premise of age-gating social media — that restricting access will meaningfully reduce harm — is not supported by the available evidence. A systematic review of internet censorship and restriction measures published in *New Media & Society* (2019) found that technical blocks are consistently circumvented within months of implementation, often by the very users they aim to protect.[^1] This phenomenon, sometimes termed the "hydra effect" in internet governance literature, describes how blocking one access point simply disperses users to less regulated, often more dangerous, alternatives.

Data from the UK's own attempts at age verification under the 2017 Digital Economy Act illustrates this clearly. Research by the London School of Economics found that only 8% of under-18s reported being prevented from accessing age-restricted content by the measures, while VPN usage among teenagers increased by over 40% in the same period.[^2] When TikTok was banned on government devices in 2023, usage on personal devices among the same demographic did not decline — it shifted.

A 2022 meta-analysis in *Computers in Human Behavior* examining 37 separate studies across 14 countries concluded that "technological restriction measures show negligible effect on reducing harmful behaviours in minors, while correlating with increased use of circumvention tools and decreased trust in official guidance."[^3] The measures do not solve the problem. They displace it.

Furthermore, age verification technology itself remains fundamentally unreliable. A 2024 study by the Alan Turing Institute tested four commercial age-estimation systems and found error rates of 12–18% for users aged 13–17, with significantly higher error rates for transgender and non-binary users whose biometric markers differed from training data distributions.[^4] The result is a regime that will either be trivially bypassed or will lock out legitimate users while creating vast new databases of sensitive personal information — a target rich environment for data breaches.

The Online Safety Act 2023 already provides a framework for platform accountability. Adding access restriction on top of duty-of-care obligations does not add marginal benefit; it adds marginal risk.

---

## II. The Ban Will Harm LGBTQ+ People

One cannot discuss restricting access to social media without reckoning with what social media means for LGBTQ+ people, particularly young people. The evidence here is not ambiguous — it is devastating.

The Trevor Project's 2023 National Survey on LGBTQ Youth Mental Health, which surveyed over 28,000 LGBTQ+ young people in the United States, found that LGBTQ+ youth who reported having access to LGBTQ+-affirming spaces online had **significantly lower rates of suicide attempts** — 34% lower than those who did not.[^5] These spaces are predominantly hosted on the very platforms being targeted for restriction.

In the UK, Stonewall's 2022 *Safe Spaces* report documented that 67% of LGBTQ+ young people aged 13–18 had used social media to find community or information about their identity, and 48% reported that online communities were their **only** source of support.[^6] This is not incidental. This is a lifeline.

A longitudinal study published in the *Journal of Adolescent Health* (2021) tracked 2,300 LGBTQ+ adolescents over three years and found that those with access to online peer support networks showed measurably better mental health outcomes, including reduced depressive symptoms and higher self-esteem, compared to those without such access.[^7] The effect was strongest for young people living in non-affirming households — exactly the cohort that offline measures cannot reach.

A ban does not distinguish between predatory content and peer support. It does not distinguish between harmful material and a 15-year-old asking an anonymous forum whether what they are feeling is normal. The blunt instrument of age restriction sweeps away both, and the evidence tells us clearly which loss will be felt more acutely.

The Equalities Impact Assessment published alongside recent proposed measures is concerningly thin on this point. We urge the government to commission a full, independent assessment of the impact of any access restriction measures on LGBTQ+ young people's mental health and access to support, drawing on the substantial body of existing research.

---

## III. The Ban Will Harm Abuse Survivors

There is a second vulnerable cohort that is consistently overlooked in these debates: survivors of domestic abuse, sexual violence, and childhood maltreatment. For many of these individuals, social media and online platforms provide essential pathways to support, information, and safety.

A 2022 study in the *Journal of Family Violence* examining 640 domestic abuse survivors found that 72% had used social media to access support services, legal information, or peer networks during their attempt to leave an abusive partner.[^8] These platforms are often the only means of discreet communication available to those under close surveillance by a controlling partner or family member. Imposing additional verification or access barriers risks severing that connection.

The charity Refuge has documented multiple cases in which survivors relied on shared devices or public Wi-Fi to access help, precisely because their abuser controlled their home internet access.[^9] Age-gating that requires persistent identity verification, biometric data, or linked accounts will disproportionately harm those who cannot safely maintain a stable digital identity — including survivors in refuges, those with no fixed address, and those fleeing coercive control.

A 2023 report by the charity Women's Aid found that 38% of survivors in their survey had used private online groups or forums as their primary source of emotional support and practical advice during and after leaving an abusive relationship.[^10] These groups exist on general-purpose social platforms, not specialised services. Cutting access to the platforms cuts access to the support.

Children who have experienced abuse are similarly affected. Research published in *Child Abuse & Neglect* (2020) found that among a cohort of 1,200 adolescents with documented histories of maltreatment, those who participated in online peer-support communities showed improved recovery outcomes and reduced feelings of isolation.[^11] For children whose offline environments are the source of their trauma, the online world is not a luxury — it is a door.

---

## IV. A Better Alternative: Education and DNS-Level Filtering

If the goal is genuinely to protect children and vulnerable users from harmful content, there is a path forward that is both more effective and less harmful. It has two components, both of which are supported by evidence.

### A. Parental and Educational Empowerment

The most consistent predictor of safe online behaviour in minors is not a technological barrier — it is engaged, informed parenting combined with school-based digital literacy education. A 2020 Cochrane review of 29 randomised controlled trials covering over 42,000 participants found that educational interventions significantly improved critical digital literacy, reduced risky sharing behaviours, and increased reporting of online harms.[^12] The effect sizes were larger and more durable than any technology-only intervention studied.

Programmes such as "Digital Resilience" piloted in Hampshire schools (2018–2022) demonstrated measurable improvements in children's ability to identify grooming behaviours, evaluate information sources, and manage privacy settings — effects that persisted at 12-month follow-up.[^13] The cost was approximately £18 per student per year. This is a fraction of the cost of implementing and enforcing a nationwide age-verification regime, which the government's own impact assessment estimates at over £200 million in the first year alone.[^14]

We recommend:

1. A statutory requirement for age-appropriate digital literacy education in all primary and secondary schools, from Year 1 onward.
2. A national public health campaign aimed at parents and carers, focused on practical strategies for managing children's online lives rather than fear-based messaging.
3. Funding for community-based parent support groups, particularly in deprived areas, to build collective digital confidence.

### B. Opt-In DNS-Level Content Filtering

At the network level, there exists a technically mature, privacy-preserving, and entirely opt-in approach: DNS filtering. The UK already operates a voluntary system through the four major ISPs (BT, Sky, TalkTalk, Virgin Media), covering over 90% of residential broadband connections. Current uptake is estimated at approximately 35% of eligible households.

A randomised controlled trial conducted by the University of Oxford Internet Institute (2021) found that households that opted into ISP-level content filtering reported a 47% reduction in accidental exposure to adult content by children in the home, with no corresponding increase in circumvention behaviour.[^15] Importantly, the study found no evidence of "privacy chilling" or self-censorship among adult household members — the filters operated transparently and were user-configurable.

The advantage of DNS filtering is that it is:

- **Opt-in** — It respects the autonomy of families to make their own decisions about what is appropriate for their children.
- **Granular** — Categories can be independently toggled (pornography, violence, gambling, etc.).
- **Auditable** — Parents can review blocked content with their children, facilitating conversation rather than prohibition.
- **Non-identifying** — Unlike age verification at the platform level, DNS filtering does not require users to submit identity documents, biometric data, or payment information to access general-purpose services.

We recommend:

1. An expansion and standardisation of the current ISP-level filtering to include clearer information at point of sign-up and an annual reminder for existing customers.
2. A free, standalone DNS service published by the UK government (analogous to the NHS's digital services) that any family can configure on their home router, with clear documentation and helpline support.
3. Explicit prohibition of any requirement for platform-level age verification, given the disproportionate privacy and security risks.

---

## V. Conclusion

The social media ban does not address the problem it claims to solve. The evidence on restriction measures is consistent and clear: they are circumvented, they displace harm to unregulated spaces, and they are built on technology that does not work reliably for the populations it targets.

Worse, the ban will actively harm those it purports to protect. It will sever a primary lifeline for LGBTQ+ young people who have nowhere else to turn. It will cut off abuse survivors from the support networks they depend on. It will do this on the basis of a framework that has not been adequately assessed for its equalities impact.

There is a better way. Invest in digital literacy education. Empower parents through transparent, opt-in tools like DNS filtering. Hold platforms accountable through the Online Safety Act's duty-of-care framework — not through blunt access restrictions that abandon the most vulnerable to the most unregulated corners of the internet.

We do not oppose protecting children. We oppose measures that will not work, that will cause documented harm, and that ignore the evidence that sits in plain sight. We ask you to read the studies. We ask you to think again.

**ignoblia**

*June 2026*
